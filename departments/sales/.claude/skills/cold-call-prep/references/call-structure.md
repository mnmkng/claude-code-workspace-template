# Call structure

The shape of a prospecting call, the segment openers, the qualification
questions, and the objections that actually come up. Used by the
`cold-call-prep` skill to build a brief; also the branch's training reference.

## The call

Six steps, about four minutes when it goes well.

| Step | Purpose | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Identify | Say who you are, where you are calling from, and that it is a sales call | 10 seconds |
| 2. Reason | Why this account, today. A trigger beats an introduction | 20 seconds |
| 3. Permission | Ask for the two minutes rather than taking them | 5 seconds |
| 4. Qualify | Three questions, no more, listening to the answers | 2 minutes |
| 5. Objection | Handle the one that comes, once. Do not argue it twice | 45 seconds |
| 6. Next step | Ask for a specific commitment with a date on it | 30 seconds |

**Say it is a sales call.** A buyer who works out in step 4 that step 1 was
evasive has stopped listening. Being straight about it is also the fastest
qualifier available: the people who stay on the line are the people worth
calling back.

**Three questions, not ten.** A call that becomes an interrogation does not get
a second one. The purpose of qualifying is to decide whether to visit, not to
build a requirements document.

## Openers by segment

### Small business

Lead with the trigger and the local fact. The office manager is not waiting for
a paper vendor, so the call has to be about something that happened.

> "Morning - this is [name] at Dunder Mifflin here in Scranton. This is a sales
> call, and it will take two minutes. We deliver next day to about forty offices
> within a few blocks of you, and I wanted to ask what you are doing for paper
> and toner right now."

What makes it work: local, specific, honest about being a sales call, and it ends
in a question about their situation rather than a claim about ours.

### Schools and counties

The purchasing officer cannot buy off a phone call, so do not try to sell on
one. The call exists to find the bid calendar and the right name.

> "Good morning - [name] at Dunder Mifflin in Scranton. I am not selling you
> anything today; procurement does not work that way. I am trying to find out
> when your paper and supplies contract next goes out to bid, and who I should be
> speaking to about the spec."

Never propose a price, a discount, or a term. Anything offered outside the bid
is a compliance problem for them, and they will remember that it was.

### Regional hospitals

The supply chain manager is measured on fill rate, substitution rate, and
invoice accuracy. Open on their scorecard, not on the relationship.

> "[name] at Dunder Mifflin, Scranton. I know you buy under a group purchasing
> agreement, so I am not calling about price. I am calling to ask whether you are
> carrying any fill-rate or substitution problems on paper, and whether we are on
> your approved vendor list."

### Lapsed account, any segment

Lead with the history. Pretending it is a first call is the fastest way to end
the conversation.

> "[name] at Dunder Mifflin. You were buying from us until [month], and the note
> on the account says [what happened]. I am not calling to talk you back into
> anything - I wanted to check whether we ever put that right."

## Qualification questions

Pick three. The first is nearly always worth asking.

| Question | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| "Who handles the supply orders day to day?" | Whether you are talking to the decision maker |
| "Roughly how many cases of copy paper do you go through in a month?" | The tier the account would land in |
| "Who are you buying from now, and how long has that been running?" | Incumbency, and whether this is winnable |
| "When did an order last arrive wrong or late?" | The single best opening for us; most accounts have a story |
| "Do you order anything else with the paper - toner, filing, breakroom?" | Basket size, and whether there is an attach opportunity |
| "Is anyone placing orders online, or is it all by phone?" | Whether Infinity is a selling point or an irrelevance |
| "When does your current arrangement come up for renewal?" | Whether to work this now or diary it |

Do not ask what they currently pay. It moves the conversation to price before
there is anything to compare against, and the number you get is usually the
headline rate on one grade.

## Objections

| Objection | Response |
|---|---|
| "We already have a supplier." | "Most people do. What I would ask is how the last problem got handled - that is usually the difference." Then a qualification question. Never argue the incumbent. |
| "You are more expensive than [a big-box retailer]." | "On copy paper, probably. Not on the rest of the basket, and I would rather compare the whole thing than one line. Can I put a full basket next to your last invoice?" |
| "Send me some information." | "I will, but the useful version takes ten minutes in person. I am out your way Wednesday - would the morning work?" Send it either way; log the send. |
| "We buy from Prince Family Paper." | "Good outfit. Where we are different is the catalog and online ordering - if you are still splitting orders across two suppliers, that is worth ten minutes." Never disparage them. |
| "No budget right now." | "Understood. When does the budget reset?" Diary it and end the call politely. |
| "Not interested." | Thank them, ask whether to take the number off the list, and mean it. Log it. |

One objection, handled once. A representative who argues the same objection
twice has turned a call that could have been a callback into a no.

## Next steps to ask for

Always specific, always with a date. "I will follow up" is not a next step.

| Segment | Ask for |
|---|---|
| Small business | A visit, named day and half-day |
| Small business, cannot get a visit | A trial delivery on one line at a quoted price |
| Schools and counties | The bid date and the purchasing officer's name |
| Regional hospitals | Confirmation of approved vendor status, and who reviews it |
| Lapsed account | Permission to send a current basket comparison, and a day to call back |
